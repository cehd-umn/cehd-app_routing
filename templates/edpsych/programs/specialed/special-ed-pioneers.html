Leading the way in special education

The special education program has a long history of influential individuals that have made it what it is today. Their contribution has not only impacted the special education program here at the University of Minnesota, but has shaped the field of special education on an international level. We remember their legacy and are grateful for their work and commitment to bettering the field of special education.

Pioneers in the field

Evelyn Deno

Evelyn Deno, born in 1911, graduated from high school at the age of 16. She continued her education at the University of Wisconsin - La Crosse to become a nursery and elementary school teacher. In the 1950s, she was an activist in state and federal policies concerning the education of children with disabilities. Dr. Deno played a crucial role in the passage of the groundbreaking Special Instruction for Handicapped Children of School Age (MN Statute 1957 131.081) Law, which required special education programs for “educable” students with disabilities. This law sparked similar state legislation across the country and ultimately resulted in the passage of the federal Education of All Handicapped Children Act, 1975. In 1958, she received a PhD in Child Psychology from the University of Minnesota. There, Dr. Deno became the first Director of Special Education for Minneapolis Public Schools. In 1966, she became the Director of the Psychoeducational Center at the University of Minnesota and Professor in Educational Psychology. She retired from the University of Minnesota in 1971. Dr. Deno passed away on June 4th, 2005.

Dr. Deno is most notably recognized for developing the “Cascade of Services” model, which remains the blueprint for the delivery of educational services today. With this approach, she advocated for an integrated, unified system of education, using an expanded continuum of service based on the needs of students. The term “cascade” refers to the cascade movement from the most fully integrated to the least integrated setting; the aim of this model is to provide services in the least restrictive environment possible. Dr. Deno’s work in this area continues to be central to federal and state special education laws.

Frank Wood

Dr. Frank Wood dedicated his life to improving outcomes for children with emotional and behavioral disabilities. His contributions to the field of special education are prolific; and yet, his career humbly began in the public-school classroom. In 1956, Dr. Wood worked as a fifth-grade, classroom teacher in the Minneapolis School District. In the classroom, Dr. Wood was drawn to teaching students with special needs; however, he felt his effectiveness as an instructor was limited due to the number of students in the general education classroom. In 1958, he left the general education classroom to become the first public school teacher for students classified as emotionally disturbed. At this time, Dr. Wood began a master’s program at the University of Minnesota where he studied mental health under Dr. Bruce Barlow and Dr. Frank B. Widerson; later, these two men, along with Dr. Stan Deno, would encourage Dr. Wood to pursue a PhD. In 1965, Dr. Wood was the first University of Minnesota PhD student to graduate with a specialization in emotional and behavioral disabilities. Following graduation, Dr. Wood became a faculty member of the Department of Educational Psychology’s Special Education Program at the University of Minnesota.

His work primarily based on developing policies, programs, and interventions for students with emotional and behavioral disorders. Dr. Wood conducted several studies on assessment, placement procedures and social behaviors of students. He was author and editor of over 120 journal articles, book chapters, and conference proceedings, and he presented over 150 presentations in professional meetings and conferences during his career. At the same time, Dr. Wood co-established the Minnesota Educators of the Emotionally Disturbed and the Minnesota Council for Children with Behavioral Disorders (CCBD). In addition to his academic responsibilities, Wood held positions at the national level, including the presidency of CCBD and the governor of both the Council for Exceptional Children (CEC) and CCBD.

Mary McEvoy

Originally from Knoxville, Tennessee, Professor Mary McEvoy received her Bachelor’s degree in Special Education and her Master’s and PhD degrees in Child and Family Studies from the University of Tennessee. She was then a researcher and professor at Vanderbilt University. Her research at Vanderbilt University focused on effective practices for including students with disabilities in early childhood settings. Her service to individuals with disabilities was not limited to young children; she was also a board member for an advocacy program for adults with disabilities. In 1990, Professor McEvoy joined the University of Minnesota’s College of Education and Human Development. During her tenure at the University of Minnesota, Professor McEvoy held positions in research, teaching, and leadership.

Professor McEvoy’s scholarly work focused on early childhood development, specifically effective inclusion for young children with disabilities. Her contributions to the area of early childhood include development of interventions to teach social interaction, as well as prevention and intervention of challenging behavior for young children (McConnell, 2004). In addition, Professor McEvoy and her colleagues developed tools such as the widely used Individual Growth and Development Indicators (IGDIs) for preschool-aged children, which were designed to measure a child’s overall development. She also contributed to the knowledge of how environmental risk and prenatal substance exposure affect developmental trajectories (McConnell, Priest, Davis, & McEvoy, 2002; Carta, Atwater, Greenwood, McConnell, McEvoy, & Williams, 2001).

Professor McEvoy served as the chair of the Department of Educational Psychology, the director of the Center for Early Education and Development (CEED), and held multiple leadership roles in national organizations such as the Division for Early Childhood of the Council for Exceptional Children. She also was involved in state and national politics for the Democratic Farm Labor Party in Minnesota, and worked with policy makers on issues such as social justice.

Until her death in 2002, Dr. Mary McEvoy made extensive contributions to early childhood development and early childhood special education. She is known for her leadership, dedication to teaching, and commitment to improving services for people with disabilities. To honor her legacy, the Division of Early Childhood and the University of Minnesota created awards such as the Light a Candle Award, Mary A. McEvoy Award for Public Engagement and Leadership, and the Mary McEvoy Service to the Field Award, for individuals who display exemplary leadership and contribute to the development of young children.

Stanley Deno

Professor Deno graduated from St. Olaf College in 1958 with a bachelor’s degree and teaching license. He worked as a high-school biology teacher for several years, before completing his PhD in educational psychology from the University of Minnesota in 1965. Professor Deno spent the next four years at the University of Delaware in his first faculty position. Initially, he focused on classroom behavior and learning, but was drawn into the field of special education. Excited about the potential to study the issues of educational inequality and to examine why certain students struggle to learn basic skills in math, writing, and reading, Professor Deno returned to the University of Minnesota in 1969. Deno’s work in educational inequality led to the development of curriculum-based measurements (CBM) in the 1980’s. Curriculum-based measurement is a widely used approach to measure the academic growth of individual students. It is an effective tool to help teachers assess and monitor student progress on learning objectives. This tool laid the foundation for important instructional practices such as data-based decision making, which allows teachers to evaluate the effectiveness of instructional methods and materials they employ. Based on research accumulated over the years, Deno provided evidence that CBM is an effective and important tool for assessing and monitoring the performance of students struggling in academic skills.

His work on CBM was extended by other researchers with regard to using CBM to screen and identify students at risk for academic failure, hold teachers and administrators accountable for student growth, evaluate attainment of learning objectives in several domains (e.g., reading, mathematics, writing), assess students who are English language learners, and measure literacy skills of Deaf and Hard of Hearing Students. Professor Deno’s passion for ensuring quality education for all students never wavered during his career, as well as throughout his life. In 2009, Dr. Deno retired from the University of Minnesota but continued to present and write about CBM and guest lecture at the university. Read more about Professor Deno's legacy on CBM here.

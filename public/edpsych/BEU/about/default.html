About the Project

Funded by a three-year grant from the National Science Foundation, “Beyond Evaluation Use” is grounded in the NSF context where well-funded, large-scale, multi-site evaluation studies are common. Long-time NSF collaborators Lawrenz and Huffman (2003) proposed a three-stage process for “multi-site, negotiated centralized” evaluations appropriate to agencies like the NSF: (1) develop local project-level evaluations, (2) negotiate a process for a centralized evaluation that will meet the needs of the funding agency, and (3) collaboratively design an integrative evaluation by combining the proposed local and central studies.

Little is known, however, about what happens when potential evaluation users at either the project or the agency level participate in designing and implementing studies or when the results become widely available at the end of project or program funding. The ultimate goal of this research is to expand the understanding of evaluation practices that promote evaluation use and influence by contrasting the type and scope of use or influence of different approaches to program evaluation in this context. Our hope is to increase understanding of how individuals and groups of people use evaluation results when a large federal agency commissions studies that have both national and local implications.

The research team, based at the University of Minnesota, is building the study on two theoretical frameworks - involvement and evaluation use/influence—to answer three broad questions: 

  1. What patterns of involvement exist in large, multi-site evaluations? 
  2. To what extent do different levels of involvement in program evaluations result in different patterns of evaluation use and influence? and
  3. What evaluation practices are most directly related to enhancing the influence of evaluations? 

 

The evaluations of four NSF programs form the programmatic content of our research:

  1. Advanced Technological Education (ATE)
  2. Math Science Partnership - Research, Evaluation and Technical Assistance (MSP-RETA)
  3. Collaboratives for Excellence in Teacher Preparation (CETP)
  4. Local Systemic Change through Teacher Enhancement (LSC)

The programs differ in the extent to which the principal investigators of funded projects were actively involved in the development and implementation of the evaluations, with ATE being the most evaluator-driven and MSP-RETA being the most client-driven. 

Research Methods

Our research methods include a formal review of literature, case studies, and a survey.  A flowchart presenting the organizational levels and data collection methods is provided here.

Review of literature. Twenty years have passed since Cousins and Leithwood (1986) wrote a comprehensive review of empirical research on evaluation use. Our research team believed it was time to update this oft-cited article by conducting a review of empirical research since 1985 to assess trends in the variables studied, methods used, and conclusions drawn. We have replicated Cousin and Leithwood’s general methods to identify research trends related to variables under study and methodological frameworks, which are reported later in this paper. In addition, we have added an assessment of the methodological quality of the studies and more detailed contextual information about the evaluations used as case material, which will be reported in later papers.

Case studies. In collaboration with the evaluations’ four principal investigators, we are developing case studies of the targeted NSF program evaluations through document analysis, interviews, and narrative responses. For each evaluation, we are preparing a summary timeline of who was involved, in what ways, and with what outcomes and perceived use and influence. This will be combined with each evaluation’s story as told by the PI, the NSF program officer(s), and selected project participants. A cross-case analysis will provide comparative information.

A survey of NSF program and project personnel. To collect data from a larger sample of individuals who participated in the four evaluations or who were familiar with their results, we have developed a survey measuring the relationship between perceptions of involvement in and influence on the evaluation process and evaluation use. The survey structure allows us to link questions in each section (i.e., the perceptions of involvement section, the influence on the evaluation process section, and the evaluation use section) to questions in the other two sections. This linking structure, one of the major challenges in developing the instrument, will allow us to study the connections between and among specific types of involvement and use. Another challenge has been to limit the survey length without reducing its completeness. The development of the survey has gone through several stages, including review by both content and survey experts, think-alouds, and pilot testing. During each of these stages we have made extensive revisions aimed at increasing the validity and reliability of the survey.

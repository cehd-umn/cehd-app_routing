David Roediger
Race and the Word: Teaching, Talking and Writing about White Supremacy 

Roediger discusses the ways in which key moments in teaching have informed his historical writings and political interventions regarding how whiteness works and how it might be challenged.  He argues that pedagogy offers strategies to expose connections between whiteness and inequality and, without lapsing into the illusion that rhetoric can move us beyond racism, to open frank dialogues encouraging thought as well as feelings about race.

Carol Miller
Shifting Shapes:  American Indian Literature and the Decolonizing of Teaching and Research

Although American Indian intellectuals have been producing contemporary literature for more than a century, the critical analysis of that body of work as a field of study is substantially younger, beginning essentially in response to the emergence of new literary voices after the publication of  N. Scott Momaday’s Pulitzer Prize-winning House Made of Dawn in the activist 1960s.  In general terms, much literary criticism of narratives by Native writers such as Momaday and Leslie Marmon Silko at first focused on how older oral traditions were being transformed and extended within print-language western genres.  A sort of second wave of critical attention tended to center on problematic identity and the dysfunctions resulting from “living in two worlds.”  Both these threads of research were productive in illuminating meaning, but both also worked from premises that assumed Western norms and Native marginality.  

Now, as Native Americans acknowledge the fallacy of post-colonial status, a new and expanding body of research, much of it by indigenous scholars, explores ways   to “decolonize” our research and teaching.  My presentation, drawn from my own study of contemporary American Indian literature, identifies the characteristics of the literary tradition developed by Native women writers over the last century as a location in which this project of decolonization has been effectively—and distinctively—engaged.  In describing how American Indian women writers have used print-language narratives in varying ways to reclaim cultural authority and continuance, I also address several of the conference’s formulating questions:   how may literature and literary analysis be deployed as disciplinary work serving the interests of Native people by defending indigenous nationhood? and how, more broadly, may this literature and the colonialist history it counters, be presented as relevant and meaningful for both Native and non-Native audiences?

Alice McIntyre
Making Whiteness A Topic of Inquiry in Teaching and Research

Educators of Color have repeatedly told white educators to rethink, reform, and restructure exclusionary practices that exist in the institutions in which we work.  They have also challenged us to be more reflective about our privileged positions as white academics and to attend to the ways in which we are implicated in the norms, standards, and educational models set by white academics and institutions. 

In this presentation, I argue that it is essential for white educators to make whiteness a central topic of inquiry in institutions of higher education.  I discuss some of my experiences as a teacher educator, and as a practitioner of participatory action research in an urban community, to illuminate the ways those of us in colleges and universities can engage in teaching and research that has the examination of whiteness at its core.  I suggest that to truly transform the ways in which white educators and predominantly white universities engage teaching, learning, and research, requires that we be open to uncertainty, take responsibility for our actions, and collaborate with others in developing strategies for transformative practice. 

Gloria Ladson-Billings
